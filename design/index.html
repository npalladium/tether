Design notes · A living prototype

The player is the stopping point.

Tether is an 8×8 puzzle about pulling three boxes into an L. Stand where a box should stop, then preserve access to the next useful position.

Preview · pull · complete

01 · Core idea

Access is the puzzle.

Walking is free. A pull brings the first visible box to the tile beside the player. Every destination therefore depends on where the player can stand now—and where they can still stand next.

02 · Inspiration

Familiar pieces, a different question.

Tether belongs to a clear lineage of compact spatial puzzles. The references below are comparisons, not novelty claims: each illuminates one part of the design while making the difference easier to name.

Sokoban · Pukoban

Avatar and boxes

Tether keeps navigation around boxes, but replaces marked destinations and one-cell pushes or pulls with long pulls to the player's position.

Ricochet Robots

Long orthogonal lines

Both reward scanning ranks and files. Here, the endpoint comes from where the player stands rather than where the environment stops a moving piece.

A Good Snowman

Construction as the goal

The objective is a recognisable arrangement, not delivery to highlighted target cells. Any L location and any rotation can win.

Rush Hour · Klotski

Access and order

Constrained movement gives choices their order dependency, while a freely walking avatar determines each pull's endpoint.

Helltaker

Short, readable sessions

A tone reference for compact puzzles with a clear finish—not a template for level count, difficulty, or mechanics.

03 · Design

Let geometry do the work.

Two verbs—walk and pull—carry the game. The interface shows the next pull without revealing the plan.

  • Shape anywhereAny three corners of a 2×2 square win, in any location or rotation.
  • Predictable pullsThe target and exact stopping tile appear before commitment.
  • Easy recoveryUndo tracks pulls, not walking. Solving matters more than matching par.

04 · Alternatives

What stayed out.

  • Aim mode and draggingBoth add input states or imply free placement. Direct box selection is clearer across pointer, touch, and keyboard.
  • Transparent pillars and auto-alignmentBoth change or bypass the core question: where can the player stand?
  • Timers, powers, and randomnessThey add burden before the existing geometry has run out of depth.

05 · Verification

How a room earns its place.

New layouts face both mechanical proof and human play.

  • Exhaust the state graphAnalysis proves the room is solvable, establishes par, counts shortest routes, and finds reachable states with no winning continuation.
  • Replay through the gameA concrete solution runs through the same movement and session rules used by the player.
  • Play for claritySolver numbers cannot show whether a choice is readable or satisfying. Playtesting has to do that.
